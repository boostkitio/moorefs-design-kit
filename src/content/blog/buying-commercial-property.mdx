If your business has been paying rent for years, buying your own premises can feel like the natural next step. You control the building, you stop paying someone else's mortgage, and over time you own an asset. It is also a bigger and slower process than buying a home, so it helps to know the steps before you start.

This guide covers buying premises your own business will trade from, which lenders call owner-occupier commercial property. If you are buying property to let to someone else, the lender's approach is different: see [commercial investment mortgages](/commercial/investment-property).

## 1. Work out what you can afford

Start with two numbers: the cash you can put in, and the repayments the business can comfortably support.

- **Cash:** most lenders want a deposit of 25% to 40% of the price. You also need money for stamp duty, legal fees, the valuation and the lender's arrangement fee. I cover this in more detail in [how much deposit you need for a commercial mortgage](/blog/commercial-mortgage-deposit).
- **Repayments:** lenders want to see the business's profits cover the new repayments with room to spare, often by around 1.3 times or more. Your current rent is a useful guide, but the lender will base its decision on your accounts.

It is worth talking to a broker at this stage, before you make an offer. Knowing roughly what lenders will offer stops you falling for a building you cannot fund.

## 2. Decide who will own the building

There are three common choices:

1. **Your trading company buys it.** Simple, and the lender assesses one set of accounts.
2. **A separate property company buys it** and lets it to your trading company. This can keep the property apart from the trading risks of the business.
3. **You buy it personally** and let it to the business.

Each choice affects tax, which lenders will help, and the guarantees they ask for. I can explain how lenders see each one, and I always recommend checking the tax side with your accountant before you commit.

## 3. Find the property and agree a price

Commercial property is marketed through commercial agents and online listings. Before you agree a price, check the basics a lender will check too: the condition of the building, its planning use class, whether it suits your trade, and whether any part of it is let.

If the building includes a flat, the rules can change. See [semi-commercial property and the 40% rule](/blog/semi-commercial-property-40-percent-rule).

## 4. Apply to the lender

Once you have an agreed price, I prepare the application. Expect lenders to ask for:

- two or three years of business accounts and recent management figures
- recent business bank statements
- details of existing borrowing
- a statement of your personal assets and liabilities
- for a newer business, a business plan with projections

The lender then gives a decision in principle, followed by credit approval. Many lenders ask directors for a personal guarantee, so it is worth understanding what that means before you sign.

## 5. Valuation

The lender instructs a chartered surveyor to value the property and check it is suitable security. You pay for the valuation, and for commercial property it costs more than a home survey. The valuation can change the loan: if it comes in below the price, the lender will usually lend against the lower figure.

## 6. Legal work and the offer

Your solicitor checks the title, searches, any leases and planning, and the lender's solicitor does its own checks. Once the lender is satisfied it issues a formal offer, sometimes called a facility letter. Read the conditions carefully, particularly any covenants about the business's finances.

## 7. Completion

On completion the deposit and the loan go to the seller's solicitor and the building is yours. From first conversation to completion usually takes a few months. If you have a fixed deadline, tell me at the start: bridging finance can sometimes cover a gap.

## Stamp duty on commercial property

In England and Northern Ireland, non-residential and mixed-use property has its own stamp duty rates. At the time of writing, GOV.UK lists 0% on the first £150,000, 2% on the next £100,000 and 5% on the rest. On a £275,000 freehold that comes to £3,250. Rates change, so check [GOV.UK's non-residential rates](https://www.gov.uk/stamp-duty-land-tax/nonresidential-and-mixed-rates) and ask your solicitor or accountant for your own figure.
